    How the index works

    The index is a teaching model, not a measurement. It adds up points for conditions that make people sick, then takes a share off for each condition that helps, then scales by your profile and your time in the street.

    index = (sum of points) × (1 − each help) × your profile × time

    • Points and percentages are ours. The research tells us the direction and roughly how strong each effect is, not exact numbers that add up. We set larger weights where the evidence is strong and small ones where it is weak.
    • Time. Sickness grows with time in a headset, roughly in a straight line, and falls with repeated sessions (Kennedy et al., 2000). We add 3% a minute, up to 60%.
    • Recovery. After exposure, sickness fades at about the rate it built up (Bos et al., 2005). Most people feel better within half an hour; some feel it for hours (Dużmańska et al., 2018).
    • Your own answer beats the estimate. The orange line on the chart is you, on the Misery Scale used in motion-sickness research (Bos et al., 2005). If it disagrees with the estimate, believe it.

      Autistic users: what the research says

      Research on VR comfort for autistic people is thin, and none of it gives numbers we could put into the index. So the index does not change for autism. These are the findings we could verify, next to the conditions they touch.

      • C5 Sound, C6 Sudden loud noise. In a survey of 4,104 autistic people, 60% had auditory over-responsivity at the time and 71% had had it at some point. A sudden horn or a soundtrack that does not fit can be far more than a startle.Carson et al., 2022 · Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders
      • C1 Camera motion, C4 Movement around you. When a field of dots rushed toward them, autistic children swayed much less than children with Asperger syndrome or typical children. Reactions to visual motion can differ, so the train illusion may land differently. The study did not measure sickness.Gepner & Mestre, 2002 · Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders
      • Every condition. Documented adverse effects of headset VR include cybersickness, anxiety and sensory disturbances, and the authors note that research on these effects in autistic users is largely absent. They recommend planning sessions to minimise them.Schmidt, Newbutt, Schmidt & Glaser, 2021 · Frontiers in Virtual Reality
      • Wearing a headset at all. In a pilot with 29 autistic adults, all were willing to wear the headset and most found it enjoyable, with a strong sense of presence.Newbutt et al., 2016 · Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders
